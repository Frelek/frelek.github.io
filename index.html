Background

Rotor blades vibrate at their natural frequencies when an excitation, such as the wakes of upstream vanes, repeats at a matching frequency. A Campbell diagram plots blade natural frequencies against rotor speed together with the engine-order excitation lines, so the crossings within the operating range can be found and checked. Vibratory stress measured or predicted at those crossings is then compared against fatigue limits, for example on a Goodman diagram that combines the alternating stress with the steady (mostly centrifugal) mean stress.

Validation of laminar aeroheating models for the Mars Science Laboratory forebody against T5 shock-tunnel data

My master's thesis: how much does a CFD heat-flux prediction for a Mars entry capsule depend on the thermal model, the numerical scheme and the wall chemistry?

Type
M.Sc. thesis, Warsaw University of Technology, Faculty of Power and Aeronautical Engineering
Year
2026
Supervisor
Dr Michał Remer
Software
ANSYS Fluent 2022 R1
Thesis (PDF) Reference: Wright et al. (2006)

The question

The heat shield of a Mars entry vehicle is sized from predicted convective heating in a hot, chemically reacting CO₂ shock layer, so it matters how sensitive those predictions are to modelling choices. Wright et al. (2006) measured heat flux on a 5 %-scale model of the MSL aeroshell, a 70° sphere-cone, in Caltech's T5 reflected shock tunnel and compared it with NASA's DPLR and LAURA codes. I took their laminar, zero-angle-of-attack shot 2254 as the benchmark and rebuilt it in ANSYS Fluent.

Three settings were varied, giving a matrix of 16 cases:

  • Spatial discretisation: first-order or second-order upwind.
  • Thermal model: one temperature (1T) or Park's two-temperature model (2T), which carries vibrational energy separately.
  • Wall: non-catalytic, supercatalytic (wall gas forced to pure CO₂), the finite-rate Mitcheltree model, and an adiabatic wall for the temperature field.

All runs are steady, laminar and 2D axisymmetric, with Fluent's density-based solver and the five-species Mars mixture (CO₂, CO, O₂, O, C; no ionisation). Specific heats come from NASA nine-coefficient polynomials and transport properties from kinetic theory. The mesh was refined at the bow shock with density-gradient adaptation.

What I found

  • The thermal model mattered more than the scheme order. With one temperature, the stagnation heat flux on catalytic walls fell well short of the reference (718–856 against about 1,100 W/cm² for the supercatalytic wall). With Park's two-temperature model, all three wall models came out close to Wright's values at the stagnation point and at the shoulder peaks.
  • Two-temperature, second order matched the reference best overall, including the rise in heating towards the shoulder. Its one drawback was some oscillation in the Mitcheltree and non-catalytic second-order solutions.
  • Wall chemistry sets the level. Non-catalytic is the lower bound and supercatalytic the upper. For this shot Mitcheltree stays much closer to the lower bound, because little atomic oxygen reaches the wall.
  • First-order solutions put the bow shock further from the body, which is visible in where the adapted mesh band sits.

As a cross-check I also worked the case by hand. A simplified Fay–Riddell estimate (perfect-gas normal shock, Sutherland viscosity, Lewis number of one) gives about 1.1 × 10³ W/cm², close to the supercatalytic CFD. The Sutton–Graves (5.6–6.1 × 10² W/cm²) and Detra–Hidalgo (5.6 × 10² W/cm²) correlations land near the non-catalytic level. You can repeat the Fay–Riddell estimate in the Aero Lab.

Model equations

The two-temperature model solves an extra conservation equation for the vibrational energy \(e_v\):

$$\frac{\partial (\rho e_v)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho e_v \mathbf{u}) = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}_v + Q_{T\text{-}V} + \sum_s \dot{\omega}_s\, e_{v,s}$$

Energy exchange between translational and vibrational modes follows Landau–Teller, with the Millikan–White relaxation time and Park's high-temperature correction:

$$Q_{T\text{-}V} = \sum_s \rho_s \frac{e_{v,s}^{*}(T) - e_{v,s}}{\tau_s}, \qquad \tau_s = \tau_s^{MW} + \tau_s^{P}$$

Dissociation rates are evaluated at Park's controlling temperature, which delays dissociation until the vibrational modes have been excited:

$$T_a = \sqrt{T\,T_v}$$

Wall heat flux, with \(n\) the wall-normal coordinate pointing into the gas, sums conduction in both energy modes and the enthalpy carried by diffusing species:

$$q_w = \kappa \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}\right)_w + \kappa_v \left(\frac{\partial T_v}{\partial n}\right)_w + \sum_s \rho D_s h_s \left(\frac{\partial Y_s}{\partial n}\right)_w$$

Starship re-entry aerodynamics

How Starship's body flaps and canards shape its aerodynamics through the descent, from M 4.05 down to subsonic speed, computed on a 15.5-million-cell mesh.

Type
M.Sc. intermediate project, Warsaw University of Technology
Title
Simulation of Reentry Conditions for Starship: A CFD Approach to Aerodynamic Coefficients
Year
2025
Supervisor
M.Sc. Eng. Michał Klamka
Software
ANSYS Fluent (density-based, k-ω SST)
Project report (PDF)

Set-up

I built a simplified Starship geometry with forward canards and aft body flaps and meshed it with a mix of structured and unstructured cells, with bodies of influence around the vehicle and its wake. A three-level mesh study (7.22, 15.5 and 16.34 million cells) showed the drag coefficient changing by less than 0.5 % between the two finest meshes, so the 15.5-million-cell mesh was used. The flow conditions follow a descent profile: twelve points from M 4.05 at 35 km to M 0.31 at 1 km, with standard-atmosphere density, temperature and viscosity at each altitude.

Results

  • The lift coefficient spikes in the transonic range (M ≈ 0.8–1.2), peaking just above M 1, then settles to a lower, steady value.
  • Drag shows the usual transonic rise, with its maximum between M 0.9 and 1.1, and falls steadily above M 2.
  • The pitching moment is slightly negative at low Mach, becomes more nose-down through the transonic range and returns towards neutral at supersonic and hypersonic speeds.

Can control-surface effects be superposed?

To avoid simulating every flap and canard combination, I tested whether their contributions add linearly. Cases with opposite deflections (fins −60° with canards +60°, and the reverse) reproduced the expected coefficients within about 10 % for the fins and 15 % for the canards. That is good enough to interpolate between configurations in a first design pass. A limitation: only one control-surface configuration was run through the whole Mach sweep.

NACA 2412 at 30°: uRANS, DES and LES compared

One stalled airfoil, three ways of treating turbulence: what each method resolves, and what it costs in mesh and time step.

Type
University project, Warsaw University of Technology
Year
2025
Supervisor
M.Sc. Eng. Michał Klamka
Software
ANSYS Fluent
Project report (PDF)

The case is a NACA 2412 with a 1 m chord at 30° angle of attack, well past stall, at M 0.3 and sea-level conditions (293 K, 101,325 Pa), which gives \(Re \approx 6.85 \times 10^6\). The domain is a C-type far field reaching 10 chords upstream and 15 downstream. Inlet turbulence intensity was 10 % in all cases.

  • uRANS (k-ω SST) averages out all turbulent fluctuations and models them. It captures the large-scale shedding cycle cheaply. Needs \(y^+ \approx 1\).
  • DES runs RANS in the attached boundary layer and switches to LES in the separated region, based on the local grid spacing. Needs \(y^+ < 5\) near the wall and fine, nearly isotropic cells in the wake.
  • LES (Smagorinsky subgrid model) resolves the large eddies everywhere and models only the smallest scales. It is the most expensive by far and needs the finest mesh near the wall.

The videos show how much more small-scale structure DES and LES keep in the wake compared with uRANS.

Falcon 9 grid fins

My bachelor's thesis: the aerodynamic characteristics of a re-entry control surface across the Mach range.

Type
B.Sc. thesis, Warsaw University of Technology
Completed
02/2024
Title
Aerodynamic Performance Analysis of Falcon 9 Grid Fins: A Computational Investigation (CFD)
Software
ANSYS (meshing, solver, CFD-Post)

Grid fins are lattices of thin intersecting plates. They give a large control force for their size and need little hinge torque, which is why Falcon 9 uses them to steer its first stage back through the atmosphere. The transonic range is the hard part: the small cells can choke, which raises drag and reduces the fin's effectiveness.

What I did

  • Built a simplified CAD model of the fin.
  • Ran CFD simulations on both 2D and 3D models and compared the results.
  • Carried out mesh-sensitivity studies (Figure 4).
  • Post-processed the flow fields in ANSYS CFD-Post.

Impinging jet heat transfer in ANSYS CFX

A round jet hitting a heated plate, rebuilt in CFX for Framatome and compared with a published STAR-CCM+ verification case.

For
Framatome
Period
10/2023 – 03/2024
Supervisor
Dr Michalina Kurkus-Gruszecka
Software
ANSYS CFX, DesignModeler
Report (PDF)

Impinging jets give very high local heat-transfer rates, but they are a tough case for turbulence models. The test case: nozzle diameter \(D = 25\) mm, nozzle-to-plate distance \(H/D = 2\), \(Re = 20{,}000\), a fully developed pipe-flow profile at the nozzle exit and a uniform heat flux on the plate. The target result is the local Nusselt number along the plate, which for \(H/D < 4\) has two peaks.

Approach

  • CFX has no true 2D mode, so I modelled the axisymmetric flow as a 5° wedge, one cell thick.
  • Prescribed the developed inlet profile with an expression based on the one-seventh power law and the Blasius friction factor.
  • Added a body of influence to refine the mesh around the nozzle exit and the stagnation region.
  • Ran k-ε as the main model and varied the wall-layer mesh across five cases (maximum \(y^+\) from about 20 to 127) to see how the Nusselt number depends on it.

Outcome

The CFX results did not reproduce the double Nusselt peak as clearly as the reference, and the values differed. Part of the reason is that CFX only offers scalable wall functions with k-ε, which limits how well the near-wall heat transfer can be resolved. My conclusion for Framatome was that this set-up in CFX is not the most accurate route for this case.

Urban wind around a new high-rise

Would adding a tall building make the streets around it too windy for pedestrians? CFD of a scaled city block, compared with wind-tunnel tests.

Type
B.Sc. intermediate project, Warsaw University of Technology
Title
Numerical Investigation of Wind Flow Patterns Around Urban Structures
Year
2023
Supervisor
Dr Marta Poćwierz
Software
ANSYS Fluent
Project report (PDF)
  • Model: the district scaled 1:500 to match the wind-tunnel model, so the results could be compared directly.
  • Inflow: the same power-law profile as in the tunnel, \(V(z) = V_m (z/10)^{\alpha}\), with \(\alpha = 0.19\) for terrain category III and 0.24 for category IV. Turbulence intensity was tuned between 15 and 35 %, and about 25 % matched best.
  • Turbulence model: realizable k-ε, constant-density air.
  • Mesh: a coarse and a fine mesh (about 12.7 million cells) with refinement around the buildings.
  • Cases: wind from 0°, 45° and 90°.

Comparison with the wind tunnel

The zones of strong wind matched the tunnel in all three directions, the strongest being next to the tallest building. The differences were local: at 45° the flow in one passage was a little faster than measured, and at 90° the CFD showed an almost stagnant zone between two buildings that the tunnel did not. I attribute the differences to mesh resolution, the k-ε constants, the inlet profile and the assumed turbulence intensity.

Fay–Riddell stagnation-point heat flux

Laminar heat flux at the stagnation point of a sphere, using the same simplified method as Chapter 4 of my thesis: perfect-gas normal shock, Newtonian velocity gradient, Sutherland viscosity.

Assumptions: steady laminar flow at the stagnation point; the shock is treated as a normal shock in a calorically perfect gas (\(\gamma = 1.30\) and \(Pr = 0.70\) for CO₂, \(\gamma = 1.40\) and \(Pr = 0.71\) for air), and the post-shock static state is used as the boundary-layer edge, as in the thesis. Strictly, the edge state is the stagnation state behind the shock; for the T5 case the difference is a few per cent. Constant pressure across the boundary layer (\(p_w = p_e\)). \(h_D\) is an input because it needs an equilibrium chemistry calculation: 0.29 for T5 is the recombination energy of the dissociated freestream (CO, O, O₂) back to CO₂, relative to \(h_0\). For flight cases, leave it at zero unless the gas behind the shock is dissociated.

Equations used

First-cell height

Atmosphere and viscosity (ideal gas, Sutherland's law):

$$\rho = \frac{p(z)}{R\,T(z)}, \qquad \mu = \mu_0\left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)^{3/2}\frac{T_0 + S}{T + S}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$

Local flat-plate skin friction and boundary-layer thickness at \(x = L\), switching at \(Re_L = 5\times10^5\) (Blasius for laminar flow, Prandtl's one-seventh power law for turbulent flow):

$$Re_L = \frac{U_\infty L}{\nu}, \qquad c_f = \begin{cases} 0.664\,Re_L^{-1/2} \\ 0.0592\,Re_L^{-1/5}\end{cases}, \qquad \delta_{99} = \begin{cases} 5.0\,L\,Re_L^{-1/2} \\ 0.37\,L\,Re_L^{-1/5}\end{cases}$$ $$\tau_w = \tfrac12 \rho U_\infty^2 c_f, \qquad u_\tau = \sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}, \qquad \Delta y = \frac{y^+\,\nu}{u_\tau}$$

Fay–Riddell

Normal-shock relations for a perfect gas:

$$\frac{p_e}{p_\infty} = 1 + \frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_\infty^2 - 1\right), \qquad \frac{\rho_e}{\rho_\infty} = \frac{(\gamma+1)M_\infty^2}{2 + (\gamma-1)M_\infty^2}, \qquad \frac{T_e}{T_\infty} = \frac{p_e/p_\infty}{\rho_e/\rho_\infty}$$

Newtonian stagnation-point velocity gradient, wall state and enthalpies:

$$\left(\frac{du_e}{dx}\right)_s = \frac{1}{R_n}\sqrt{\frac{2\,(p_e - p_\infty)}{\rho_e}}, \qquad \rho_w = \frac{p_e}{R\,T_w}, \qquad h_{0e} = c_p T_\infty + \tfrac12 V_\infty^2, \quad h_w = c_p T_w$$

Heat flux (Fay and Riddell, 1958), with the dissociation factor \(\Phi\) depending on the boundary-layer and wall assumption:

$$q_w = 0.763\,Pr^{-0.6}\,(\rho_e\mu_e)^{0.4}\,(\rho_w\mu_w)^{0.1}\,\sqrt{\left(\frac{du_e}{dx}\right)_s}\;(h_{0e} - h_w)\;\Phi$$ $$\Phi_{\text{equilibrium}} = 1 + \left(Le^{0.52} - 1\right)\frac{h_D}{h_{0e}}, \qquad \Phi_{\text{frozen, catalytic}} = 1 + \left(Le^{0.63} - 1\right)\frac{h_D}{h_{0e}}, \qquad \Phi_{\text{frozen, non-catalytic}} = 1 - \frac{h_D}{h_{0e}}$$

Total-pressure ratio across the normal shock (Rayleigh pitot formula):

$$\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)M_\infty^2}{2 + (\gamma-1)M_\infty^2}\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\left[\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma M_\infty^2 - (\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}$$

This project wasn’t just another CFD run over a wing. I went deep—real deep—into the aerodynamic chaos zone to see what really happens when air decides to flip the script. My mission: take the classic NACA 2412 airfoil at Re=6.85×10⁶, pump three turbulence strategies (uRANS, DES, LES), and let them battle it out under full-on separation conditions. Why? To figure out who really understands vortex drama, boundary layer plot twists, and wake formation during airflow stress tests.

Here’s how I rolled:

CFD Turbulence Showdown:
  • uRANS: Fast, efficient, but misses out on the wild turbulence party.
  • DES: Middle ground, grabs some of the action.
  • LES: The truth teller—shows every detail if your hardware can handle the heat.
Mesh Crafting:

I built every mesh from scratch. Tuned y⁺ to about 1 (for uRANS/DES), dialed it below 5 for LES. Stretched those inflation layers so wake regions hit the Taylor micro/kolmogorov scales—basically, zoomed in on the tiniest energy transfers happening in turbulent zones.

Solver Moves:

Ran a density-based scheme with CFL-driven timesteps—never let solver stability drop the beat.

Epic Aerodynamic Phenomena Observed:

  • Vortex shedding: Watched air break up into swirling madness behind the airfoil.
  • Boundary layer detachment/reattachment: Pinpointed exactly where the flow decided to chill or bounce back—classic aerodynamic "plot twists."
  • Wake formation: Saw the birth and evolution of those wake regions, different for every model.
  • Kolmogorov scale resolution: LES ran close enough to see energy cascading down to the tiniest scales—actual turbulence, not just numbers.
  • Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities: Spotted those shear layer roll-ups—major vibe in aeroacoustics and real-world drag.
  • Hardware performance juggling: Ran full-second, high-fidelity LES on a classic setup; learned when to push, pause, and post-process to avoid burnout.

What I Learned:

  • Turbulence strategy mastery: Know when and why each model shines (or flops)—critical for reentry vehicles, rocket separation, or any high-stakes aerodynamic drama.
  • Mesh artistry: If you want to see real turbulence, you build the mesh with love and grind for those Kolmogorov vibes in separation zones.
  • Boundary layer attitude: Achieving y⁺ ≈ 1 and tracking transitions gave me pro instincts for wall modeling.
  • Turbulence intuition: Now, I can look at vorticity patterns and tell you when things get real—way better than trusting residuals alone.
  • Smart engineering communication: Turning millions of cells and mega turbulence data into visuals and stories people actually get.

This CFD journey taught me how to own complexity, wrangle hardware, and translate raw aerodynamic data into decision-worthy insights. Ready to tackle any flow challenge, from high-speed reentry to next-gen rocket dynamics—because, in the end, it’s all about catching the real turbulence, not just the math.

uRANS, DES, and LES: Which One Tells the Truth?

This project started because I was genuinely curious about something: when airflow gets messy around an airfoil, which CFD approach actually tells you the truth? So I grabbed the classic NACA 2412, cranked the Reynolds number to 6.85 × 106, and put three different turbulence models through their paces—uRANS, DES, and LES.

The whole point wasn't just to run simulations. I wanted to see what happens when air decides to separate, swirl, and cause problems. You know, the stuff that makes engineers lose sleep when designing real aircraft.

The Real Talk Comparison:

Model Mesh Requirements What You Actually See Reality Check When to Use It
uRANS y⁺ ≈ 1 Basic separation, averaged flow Fast but misses details Quick checks, early design
DES y⁺ ≈ 1 Big separation features, some dynamics Good middle ground When you need some detail
LES y⁺ < 5 Everything—full vortex chaos, energy cascades The whole truth (RIP your computer) Critical flows, research

Building the Setup:

I spent weeks crafting meshes for each model. Got y⁺ down to about 1 for the wall-resolved stuff, pushed it under 5 for LES. Those inflation layers? Hand-tuned so I could catch the tiny energy transfers happening in the chaotic zones—we're talking Kolmogorov scale territory here. Then I fired up a density-based solver with time steps that actually made sense for the physics.

What I Actually Saw:

  • Vortex madness: Real vortex streets peeling off the airfoil—not just pretty pictures, but the actual mechanisms that create drag and noise on every plane you've ever flown.
  • Flow breakups: Watched exactly where the boundary layer said "nope" and detached, then tracked if and when it decided to stick back on. This stuff determines when wings stall.
  • Wake chaos: Each model showed me different wake structures. LES was wild—I could literally see energy cascading from big swirls down to tiny eddies.
  • Shear layer drama: Caught those Kelvin-Helmholtz roll-ups that textbooks love to mention but you rarely see in action.
  • Computer battles: LES pushed my setup so hard I had to get creative with memory management and file saves. Nothing like watching your CPU temperature while chasing turbulence truth.

What Stuck With Me:

  • Now I can look at a flow problem and know which turbulence model will actually work—not just pick the fanciest one.
  • Developed a weird intuition for reading vorticity plots. I can spot when a simulation is lying versus when it's showing real physics.
  • Got obsessed with mesh quality. If you want honest results, you build honest meshes.
  • Learned that the best CFD work means nothing if you can't explain what you found to other people.

This whole experience taught me that turbulence modeling isn't just about picking settings—it's about understanding what's really happening in the flow. Whether it's a reentry vehicle hitting atmosphere or a rocket separating stages, I now know how to dig into the physics and figure out what's actually going on.

Impinging Jet

Download PDF
Tags: #ImpingingJet #CFX #UDF #Pseudo2D #TurbulenceModels #Validation

🧪 I took on an impinging jet CFD project where I customized the velocity inlet using my own User-Defined Function (UDF). I tried out different turbulence models—each with their own oddities—and compared the outcomes with published data and other CFD tools to see who really had the best predictions.

🧩 Since ANSYS CFX doesn't really handle pure 2D simulations, I got around that by squeezing my grid to just one cell wide—basically faking a 2D setup so I could still compare my results to what’s out there in the literature and experiments.

🧵 A big takeaway for me: the mesh matters—a lot. Depending on how fine or coarse the grid was, I’d see huge changes in my results, and the turbulence models could tell very different stories about the jet’s behavior. It was a real eye-opener for how critical it is to choose the right setup, and I also bumped into a few quirks and limits with CFX along the way.

What came out of it?

  • 🔍 First, I saw firsthand how mesh quality can make or break your results.
  • 🌪️ I got a clearer sense of how turbulence models behave and why it’s worth trying more than one—sometimes they disagree a lot.
  • 🛠️ I now know how to set up and run custom UDFs, making simulations much more flexible.
  • 📏 It was also a real exercise in checking and benchmarking results—comparing with published data taught me a lot about what to trust (and what not to).

Lessons learned

  • ✍️ Writing my own velocity-inlet UDF let me really control the inflow and keep things repeatable.
  • 📐 Going “pseudo-2D” in CFX is a neat trick if you want to run 2D-style cases in a software that’s not built for it.
  • 🧵 The turbulence model you pick seriously changes your results—SST, for example, gave different predictions for heat transfer and flow details near the wall compared to some others.
  • 🧱 Mesh around the jet core and stagnation point needs to be tight if you want stable and accurate results.
  • 🧪 Double-checking everything with published work and other CFD packages made it clear where CFX shines—and where it doesn’t.

🚀 In the end, the project was a deep dive into practical CFD, code tweaks, and getting creative with software. I learned a lot by trying, failing, adapting, and then testing everything against real-world data—not just trusting what the computer spits out.

Aeromechanics Engineering Experience

During my time as an aeromechanics engineer at the Warsaw Institute of Aviation collaborating with GE Aerospace, I focused exclusively on commercial aircraft engine compressors and turbines. My role combined computational analysis with real-world test data to ensure blade reliability and engine safety under demanding flight conditions.

Campbell Diagram Analysis for Engine Rotating Assemblies

I routinely generated Campbell diagrams for compressor and turbine blade rows to pinpoint where blade natural frequencies intersected with engine excitation harmonics. By mapping these intersections across the speed range, I identified critical speed bands that could trigger forced response or flutter. This work enabled design adjustments—such as tweaking blade mass or stiffness—to steer clear of dangerous resonance zones without compromising efficiency.

Modal Analysis of Compressor and Turbine Blades

My modal analysis characterized how individual compressor and turbine blades vibrate under aerodynamic and thermal loads. For compressor stages, I examined how inlet distortions and stage interactions influenced blade modes. For turbine stages, I incorporated temperature‑dependent material properties to capture shifts in natural frequencies during operation. Validating these models against blade tip‑timing and strain‑gauge measurements ensured accurate prediction of vibrational behavior across engine sections.

Fatigue Life Assessment Using Goodman Diagrams

To predict blade service life, I applied Goodman diagram techniques tailored for aircraft engine conditions. By plotting alternating stress amplitudes from vibration against mean stresses from centrifugal and thermal loads, I could estimate high cycle fatigue (HCF) life for both compressor and turbine blades. This approach supported maintenance planning by defining safe inspection intervals based on realistic loading scenarios.

High Cycle Fatigue (HCF) Analysis

My fatigue work focused solely on high cycle fatigue (HCF), addressing the elastic‑deformation cycles induced by steady‑state engine vibrations. Using experimental data from operational engines and test rigs, I refined damage‑accumulation models to improve life‑prediction accuracy. This ensured blade designs met GE’s stringent durability requirements under continuous high‑frequency loading.

Integrated Computational and Experimental Workflow

I combined CFD‑based aerodynamic loading with finite element (FE) structural models to simulate compressor and turbine blade dynamics within full-engine assemblies. These multiphysics simulations captured interactions between adjacent blade rows and accounted for real boundary conditions such as inlet pressure distortions and combustor exit temperature patterns. All results were rigorously validated against full‑scale engine tests and component‑level rig data to confirm model fidelity.

Field Issue Investigations

A highlight of my work was leading investigations into real engine issues reported by operators—like unexpected compressor blade cracking on high‑flight‑cycle engines. By correlating field vibration data with wear patterns, I identified root causes (e.g., particle erosion amplifying vibratory stress) and recommended mitigation measures such as updated blade coatings and optimized inspection schedules. These solutions directly improved engine reliability and reduced unscheduled maintenance events.

This hands-on experience with aircraft engine compressors and turbines honed my skills in vibration analysis, fatigue life prediction, and multiphysics modeling—expertise that’s directly applicable to advancing the safety and performance of modern jet engines.

Aerodynamic Engineering Internship at Rocket Factory Augsburg

During my internship at Rocket Factory Augsburg (RFA) in Augsburg, I handled aerodynamic design and analysis for the RFA ONE launch vehicle across the core flight regimes, using ANSYS Fluent and Python automation to streamline workflows.

Full-Flight Mach Regime Analysis

I ran CFD simulations in ANSYS Fluent for:

  • Subsonic (M < 0.8): Mapped pressure distributions during liftoff roll and early ascent.
  • Transonic (0.8 < M < 1.2): Investigated shock–boundary layer interactions over the nose cone and interstage fairings, optimizing shapes to delay separation peaks.
  • Supersonic (1.2 < M < 5): Studied shock patterns and expansion fans on the stainless-steel airframe, ensuring stability through Mach-dependent center-of-pressure shifts.

These studies highlighted transonic separation hotspots and aftward shifts in aerodynamic center at supersonic speeds.

RFA ONE Launch Vehicle

Plume-On vs. Plume-Off Analysis

I compared engine-on and engine-off cases in Fluent to capture plume–body interactions:

  • Plume-On: Modeled exhaust impingement and thermal effects on the aft section, revealing recirculation zones that impact base drag and local heating.
  • Plume-Off: Established clean-air baselines for aerodynamic coefficients, crucial for control-law development during engine coast phases.

Results led to minor aft-body contour tweaks to minimize plume-induced recirculation and thermal stress.

Python Automation for Post-Processing

I built Python scripts to parse Fluent output, automatically extracting:

  • Surface pressure (Cp) distributions
  • Aerodynamic force and moment coefficients
  • Shock location metrics

Automated plotting and report generation accelerated result analysis and design feedback.

Key Aerodynamic Insights

  • Shock-Boundary Layer Interaction: Improved fairing transitions to reduce drag spikes in transonic flight.
  • Center-of-Pressure Migration: Adjusted fin sizing and mass distribution to maintain stability at varying Mach.
  • Plume-Induced Flow Fields: Refined nozzle-aft contours to smooth recirculation zones, enhancing separation reliability.

This internship sharpened my expertise in ANSYS Fluent simulations, Python-driven post-processing, and comprehensive Mach-regime aerodynamics—skills that directly support cutting-edge launch vehicle development.

Bachelor Thesis: Falcon 9 Grid Fin Aerodynamics

Tags: #SST k-w #2D #3D #symmetry #Density based

My Bachelor thesis, titled "Aerodynamic Performance Analysis of Falcon 9 Grid Fins: A Computational Investigation", was a deep-dive CFD study into the aerodynamics of reusable rocketry. I analyzed the flow behavior around SpaceX’s Falcon 9 titanium grid fins across subsonic, transonic, and supersonic regimes, using ANSYS Fluent and the SST k-ω turbulence model.

I developed high-fidelity 2D and 3D CFD models of the grid fin mounted on the booster stage, executed mesh sensitivity studies, and extracted aerodynamic coefficients (drag, lift, moment) across multiple Mach numbers. Realistic conditions were modeled based on actual Falcon 9 reentry altitudes and speeds, and the simulations revealed valuable insights about shockwave interaction, flow separation, and boundary layer behavior.

While the simulations were limited to Mach 1.91 due to hardware constraints, I ensured full convergence and physical accuracy through optimized meshing, inflation layers, and adaptive solver control. The final results showed how the back-swept design of SpaceX's titanium fins significantly improves supersonic performance and delays wave drag onset—a key factor for reentry control.

🚀 Lessons Learned

  • Mastering mesh quality: By tuning inflation layers for a y⁺ around 1, I learned firsthand how a well‑constructed mesh can dramatically improve shock capturing and solution stability in supersonic flows.
  • Modeling high‑speed air: Setting up correct boundary conditions and gas properties for Mach >1 taught me the nuances of real‑gas effects and how to predict bow‑shock angles with confidence.
    • Bow‑shock familiarity: Watching shock waves wrap around grid fins showed me the practical side of oblique‐shock theory—and inspired me to explore control‑surface interactions in highly sonic conditions.
  • Deepening CFD theory: Building everything solo—from geometry cleanup to solver setup—gave me a solid grasp of turbulence models (SST k‑ω), convergence criteria, and when to trust residuals versus physical trends.
    • Independent problem‑solving: Tackling mesh refinement, solver crashes, and post‑processing challenges alone honed my resilience—and taught me to document each step so future peers (or employers) can pick up where I left off.
  • Communicating complexity: Presenting my results to faculty members sharpened my ability to translate dense CFD data into clear, actionable insights—an essential skill for any collaborative engineering environment.

🚀 Aerodynamic Performance Analysis of Starship Reentry

Warsaw University of Technology (WUT)

I conducted an extensive computational study on Starship's aerodynamic performance during atmospheric reentry, focusing on how its body shape, forward canards, and aft flaps interact to control descent stability, lift, and drag under extreme conditions. The work combined high‑fidelity CFD simulations (with a 15.5‑million‑cell mesh validated through sensitivity studies) using ANSYS Fluent to accurately resolve shock‑wave structures and boundary‑layer dynamics throughout subsonic, transonic, and hypersonic flow regimes.

🧪 Study Objective & Method

  • Mapped how angle of attack and control‑surface deflections affect forces and moments across reentry regimes where loads and heating are critical.
  • Simulated a matrix of canard/flap settings—both independently and in counterposition.
  • Confirmed that a superposition principle reliably predicts combined effects, reducing compute cost while maintaining accuracy.
  • Executed mesh‑independence and solution‑convergence checks.

📊 CFD Setup Highlights

  • Mesh: 15.5M cells, refined shock/BL resolution; prism layers tuned for y+ targets.
  • Regimes: Subsonic → Transonic → Hypersonic; density‑based solver with regime‑appropriate CFL control.
  • Physics: Shock capturing, separation/reattachment behavior, control‑surface hinge‑line effects.
  • Post‑processing: Automated force/moment extraction, Cp lines, L/D maps, trim analysis.

🔧 Control‑Surface Matrix & Trim

  • Quantified lift surges, transonic drag rise, and trim requirements (Cm trends, center‑of‑pressure migration).
  • Identified optimal canard/flap strategies for controllability, heat load management, and reduced post‑flight wear.
  • Established trim tables for guidance and entry corridor targeting.

✅ Validation & Deliverables

  • Compared trends against available flight‑test data and literature benchmarks.
  • Delivered a reusable aerodynamic database (AoA × deflections × Mach) and Python tools for interpolation and envelope checks.
  • Provided guidance‑law inputs and control‑surface schedules for closed‑loop reentry concepts.

📈 Impact for Rocket Employers

  • Cost & schedule: Superposition‑based prediction cut compute time while preserving fidelity.
  • Risk reduction: Early detection of transonic drag spikes and trim gaps informs heat‑load margins and actuator sizing.
  • Ops readiness: Clean handoff artifacts (ADB + scripts) for GNC, testing, and flight readiness reviews.

🔭 What’s Next

  • Expand flap/canard matrices; incorporate wind‑tunnel and flight‑test cross‑validation.
  • Integrate ADB into trajectory optimization and entry guidance algorithms.
